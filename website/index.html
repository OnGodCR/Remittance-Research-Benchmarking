The study Results Replay a test

A student research project, summer 2026

Can a blockchain carry the world's remittances?

People working abroad send home roughly $905 billion a year. At the World Bank's standard $200 payment, that averages out to about 150 payments every second, worldwide, with paydays and holidays pushing well above that. We built three kinds of blockchain payment network, sent them realistic payment traffic, and measured how many payments each could settle, how long it took, and what it cost.

  1. The question

    Which way of making a blockchain faster — a sidechain, an off-chain payment network, or sharding — would best raise how many payments a remittance system can settle, and lower what each one costs?

    Sidechain: Polygon Edge

    A separate, smaller blockchain run by a small group of computers that take turns approving batches of payments. Fewer computers have to agree, so they can agree faster.

    Sharding: NEAR

    One blockchain split into several lanes ("shards") that work at the same time, like opening more checkout counters in a shop.

    Off-chain: Lightning

    Two people lock money into a shared account once, then pass it back and forth privately. Payments hop between accounts like this to reach someone further away, and only the opening and closing go on the blockchain.

  2. Why we built our own networks

    Each of these systems has a free public "practice" network. We tried those first and rejected them: they are shared with strangers and limit how fast you can send. A slow result could mean the design is slow, or just that someone else was busy, and there is no way to tell which.

    So we rented computers from Google Cloud and ran the real production software each network uses, on private networks only we could touch. That gave us full control, at the cost of building everything ourselves. We spent about $124 of a $300 credit in total.

  3. Making it realistic

    A blockchain on one rack of computers in one building would be unrealistically fast. We added seven layers of realism, one at a time, checking the results still made sense after each:

    • Four real regions on remittance routes. Virginia (the largest sending country), Mumbai (the largest receiving country), Singapore (the Philippines and South-East Asia route) and London (a major sender to South Asia). The computers running each blockchain were spread across all four, so they really had to talk across oceans.
    • Extra delay, jitter and lost messages, set from published internet measurements for each route, added on top of the real distance.
    • Background traffic. A second program sent ordinary, non-remittance transactions alongside ours, about 88 a second, matching how busy the real Polygon network was.
    • Real hardware sizes. Each computer had 8 processor cores and 32 GB of memory, the published recommendation for running these networks.
    • Computers failing. In a separate set of tests, we switched one of the computers running the blockchain off and on during the test, to see what an outage does.
    • Checked against the real networks. We compared our traffic and capacity to what the real public networks actually carry.
    • Every test repeated five times, so we report ranges rather than a single lucky number.

  4. How one test works

    5 minutes, random timing

    Payments arrive at random moments at a set average rate, the way real customers do, rather than on a fixed beat. Each test lasts 300 seconds.

    A ramp of 7 load levels

    10, 25, 50, 100, 150, 200 and 300 payments a second. Each test is sent at one level, at each of four amounts: $10, $50, $200 and $500.

    Strict rules for "settled"

    A payment only counts if it is final within 60 seconds. Past that, a remittance has failed whatever the chain does later. The first 10 seconds of every test are ignored while connections warm up.

    Polygon and NEAR moved a test stablecoin, a token always worth $1, because that is what real remittances on those networks use. Lightning moved bitcoin, because basic Lightning has no stablecoin. So a Lightning remittance carries exchange-rate risk the other two don't.

  5. Cleaning the data, with the rules written first

    Before any real data existed, on 7 August 2026, we wrote down the rules for which tests would count. For example, a test is thrown out if our own sending program fell behind schedule, or if a computer was reclaimed mid-test. Writing the rules first means we couldn't quietly drop results we didn't like.

    Nothing is ever deleted. Every thrown-out test stays in the raw data with the rule that removed it, so anyone can check the two against each other.

  6. What we found

    • Polygon Edge settled 200 payments a second with no failures, 95% of them within 4.8 seconds.
    • NEAR settled 150 a second with 0.15% failures, but slowly: 95% took up to 19.2 seconds, and none took under about 8. Part of that delay is our setting. NEAR's default 0.6-second block timer could not keep four continents in agreement, so we slowed it to 2 seconds. A small follow-up (only one or two surviving tests) at 1.2 seconds cut the typical wait from 12.2 to 7.2 seconds.
    • Pushed past their limits, both fall over. NEAR falls faster: at twice its peak load it delivered about 7% of its peak, against about 33% for Polygon. Whether part of NEAR's collapse at 200 a second and above is our own measuring program, rather than NEAR, is still being checked.
    • Sharding did not help a stablecoin. Every token transfer had to run in the one lane that holds the token's program, so adding lanes cannot speed it up.
    • All three are cheap per $200 payment: NEAR $0.000365, Polygon $0.0006, Lightning $0.0523. Lightning's fee grows with the amount, so at $500 it passes our 10-cent limit.
    • Lightning's real problem is reliability. A published study of the real network (Waugh & Holz, 2020) found success falling from 72% for one-cent payments to 17% at $100. Our test network was about 620 times smaller than the real one, so we use their measurement rather than ours.
    • The rule we fixed in advance picks Polygon Edge: the highest load sustained while 95% of payments settle within 10 seconds and cost stays under 10 cents. That verdict depends on NEAR's block timer, as explained above.

  7. What this cannot tell you

    • These are best-case estimates from controlled, partly realistic networks, not a copy of the real public networks.
    • We tested settling the payment only, not converting cash to and from crypto at each end, which may be the hardest part of a real remittance.
    • Connecting to bank systems like SWIFT is discussed in the paper's design proposal; nothing was built.
    • One product per design. NEAR stands in for sharding, Polygon Edge for sidechains. Polygon Edge was frozen by its makers in December 2024; we used its final version on purpose.
    • NEAR ran 4 lanes with 2 computers each, the minimum allowed, because of our cloud account's limit on processor cores.

Replay a test

This is not a live blockchain. Every replay is re-drawn from payments we actually recorded, so it shows what our networks did, and nothing beyond. Combinations we never tested are greyed out, because guessing between measured levels is exactly where a system can fall off a cliff.

Whole runs recommended

First pick one of the five recorded tests at random, then draw payments from inside it. Payments in the same test shared the same network conditions and the same slow moments, so they belong together. Keeping them together keeps the honest test-to-test variation. The formal name is cluster sampling.

Individual payments

Pour every payment from all five tests into one pile and draw each one independently, like names from a hat. This is simple random sampling. It mixes moments from different tests, so the results look smoother and more certain than the evidence supports. Compare the two below.
